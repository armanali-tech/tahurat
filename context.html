Context of Practice

This inquiry is situated within my Grade 6 Science classroom in Mumbai within the ICSE curriculum context. The classroom consists of learners with diverse academic abilities, confidence levels, learning preferences, and language proficiencies. While some students actively participated during discussions and responded confidently to questions, others hesitated to contribute, particularly during whole class questioning.

Through observations and assessment analysis, I noticed that many students were comfortable recalling factual information but struggled when asked to explain concepts, justify answers, or apply understanding to unfamiliar situations.

Curriculum Context

The ICSE curriculum places significant emphasis on conceptual understanding, application of knowledge, and interdisciplinary learning. However, despite these expectations, many classroom interactions remained surface level, with students focusing primarily on memorisation rather than deeper thinking.

Key Challenges

  • Unequal participation during questioning
  • Varying levels of confidence among learners
  • Difficulty in expressing scientific ideas
  • Limited engagement with analytical thinking
  • Students with strong verbal skills dominating discussions while quieter learners remained passive

The Water Unit

The Water Unit was selected as the focus of this inquiry because it is both relatable and conceptually rich. Topics such as water sources, the water cycle, water pollution, and conservation provide opportunities for students to connect scientific understanding to everyday life experiences. At the same time, these topics encourage explanation, reasoning, and critical thinking.

This context also presented practical challenges such as limited time for extended discussion, varying levels of vocabulary knowledge, and differences in learner confidence. These realities highlighted the importance of designing questioning strategies that are both cognitively challenging and accessible to all learners.

Linguistic Diversity

Another important contextual factor was the linguistic diversity within the classroom. Some learners were highly confident in spoken English, while others needed additional time to process scientific vocabulary before contributing verbally. This became particularly visible during higher order questioning tasks where students appeared to understand concepts but struggled to articulate responses using formal scientific language.

Reflecting on this helped me recognise that silence in the classroom cannot always be interpreted as lack of understanding. In many cases, it reflected hesitation, fear of making mistakes, or difficulty expressing complex ideas publicly. I realised that if questioning only privileges the fastest or most confident learners, then it risks excluding a large portion of the class from meaningful scientific dialogue.
